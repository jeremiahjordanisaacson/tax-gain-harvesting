What is Tax-Gain Harvesting?

The federal government taxes long-term capital gains (investments held more than a year) at a special 0% rate — as long as your taxable income stays below a threshold. Tax-gain harvesting is the deliberate act of selling appreciated investments in a low-income year to realize those gains while they land inside that 0% bracket, then (optionally) buying back in to raise your cost basis. You pay $0 federal tax on the gain, and money that would've gone to taxes stays invested and compounding.

The core idea in one sentence

In a low-income year, sell appreciated long-term stock to realize gains that fit inside the 0% long-term capital-gains bracket, pay $0 federal tax on them, then — if you like — buy back in to reset your cost basis higher. Repeat in future low-income years.

🌾 Why "harvesting"?

You spend your working years planting and growing a portfolio. Then, in the right season — a low-income year — you harvest the gains at 0%. Like a farmer picking the ripe crop and replanting the field, you realize the gain, reset your basis, and let it grow again.

The 0% Bracket — and the Standard Deduction Comes First

The 0% threshold is based on taxable income — i.e. income after your deduction. So you generally get the standard deduction first, and then the 0% capital-gains bracket stacks on top of that. With essentially no other income, the two numbers add together into a surprisingly large amount of gain you can realize tax-free.

A married couple with basically no other taxable income could realize roughly $131,100 of long-term profit at a 0% regular federal rate — not "$98,900," because the $32,200 standard deduction comes first.

These numbers move every year

The dollar limits are inflation-adjusted annually. Don't assume the 2026 figures are identical in 2027, 2028, and beyond — re-check each year before you harvest.

Gain ≠ Proceeds (This Is the Whole Trick)

The 0% bracket limits your capital gain — not the amount of stock you sell. Your gain is only the profit above what you paid (your cost basis). The original money you invested is a tax-free return of capital.

capital_gain = sale_proceeds − cost_basis

Worked example — selling $200,000 of stock

You sold $200,000 of stock but only realized a $100,000 gain. The other $100,000 was your basis coming back to you, untaxed.

The consequence

Depending on your cost basis, you may be able to sell far more stock than the gain limit. A married couple filling the ~$131,100 of 0% gain room in 2026 might sell several hundred thousand dollars of actual stock, because only the profit portion counts as the gain. That's what makes the strategy so powerful.

Why the "gap years" are gold

The years after you stop earning a salary but before large pensions, Social Security, and required minimum distributions (RMDs) begin are often your lowest-income years ever. That empty space under the 0% ceiling is prime harvest ground. A couple who retires near year-end can start the next calendar year with little or no earned income and harvest aggressively.

Step 1 — Realize the Gain

In your low-income year, sell appreciated shares you've held more than a year, realizing an amount of gain that fits inside your 0% room. The sale is a taxable event, but if the gain lands in the 0% bracket, the regular federal tax on it is $0.

Worked example — a $130,000 harvest (married filing jointly, 2026)

Step 2 — Reinvest & Reset Your Basis

Here's the part that makes harvesting different from simply spending the money: you don't have to spend it. You can buy the investment right back. Your new cost basis becomes roughly today's price — so you've converted previously unrealized appreciation into fresh basis at a 0% tax cost.

Continuing the example

old_basis = $100,000
harvested_gain = $130,000 (taxed at 0%)
rebuy at current value → new_basis ≈ $230,000

You turned $130,000 of unrealized appreciation into additional cost basis while paying $0 federal capital-gains tax. If the stock later keeps climbing, you've permanently removed that first $130,000 from any future taxable gain. Do it again in the next low-income year.

🌾 The wash-sale rule is generally not your problem here

The wash-sale rule disallows a loss when you rebuy a substantially identical security within 30 days. When you're intentionally realizing a gain, there's no loss to disallow — so you can typically sell and rebuy immediately. (This is the opposite of tax-loss harvesting, where the 30-day rule forces you to wait or swap into something different.)

Why this means more money over time

It's not magic growth — the investment doesn't earn more. It's that the capital you didn't hand over in taxes stays invested and keeps compounding. If you'd instead sold a big appreciated position during a high-income year, some of that gain could be taxed at 15%, 20%, plus the 3.8% NIIT on top. Harvesting slices at 0% keep more of your capital working for you.

The Math That Matters

Stacking order: ordinary income fills the bracket first

Think of your income as water filling a tank from the bottom. Your deduction carves out the tax-free bottom. Then ordinary income (wages, interest, pension, taxable withdrawals, Roth conversions) fills the brackets first. Your long-term gains and qualified dividends stack on top — and only the portion that sits below the 0% ceiling gets the 0% rate.

How much gain fits at 0%?

A clean way to estimate your gross long-term gain room at 0% in a year:

room_at_0% = (zero_bracket_top + standard_deduction) − other_ordinary_income − qualified_dividends
(floored at $0)

With zero other income, that's just zero_bracket_top + standard_deduction — e.g. MFJ 2026: $98,900 + $32,200 = $131,100. Every dollar of ordinary income or qualified dividends you have that year shrinks the room dollar-for-dollar.

The Gifting Play 🎁

There's a related move. Say you own highly appreciated stock and genuinely want to give some to, say, your adult nephew. Instead of selling it yourself (and realizing the gain in your bracket), you can gift the stock. Receiving a legitimate gift generally isn't taxable income to the recipient — but the gain doesn't vanish.

Carryover basis — the key mechanic

With appreciated gifted stock, the recipient generally takes your cost basis (and tacks on your holding period). The embedded gain travels with the shares.

FieldValue
Your cost basis$5,000
Value when gifted$35,000
Nephew's basis (carryover)~$5,000
He sells for $35,000 → his gain~$30,000

Now his tax situation governs. If he's an independent adult with little other income and the holding is long-term, that ~$30,000 gain could fall inside his 0% bracket.

⚠️ The "kiddie tax" trap

Don't assume a young person automatically gets their own 0% bracket. The kiddie tax can tax a child's unearned income at the parents' rates. It isn't simply "under 18" — it can apply to someone who is:

  • Under 18; or
  • Age 18 and doesn't provide more than half their own support with earned income; or
  • A full-time student age 19–23 who doesn't provide more than half their own support with earned income.

Gifting appreciated stock to a 20-year-old college student and assuming they'll use their own 0% bracket can backfire.

Gift-tax basics (2026)

  • Annual exclusion: one person can generally give another $19,000 per year with no reporting. A married couple can give the same person $38,000 using both spouses' exclusions.
  • That's not a legal maximum. You can give more. Exceeding the annual exclusion generally means the donor files Form 709 and dips into their lifetime exemption — it rarely means anyone actually owes gift tax.
  • Lifetime exemption: $15,000,000 per person in 2026. A $50,000 gift doesn't make anyone suddenly owe gift tax.
  • Gift tax is the donor's issue, not the recipient's.

It has to be a real gift

Once you give someone stock, it's genuinely their property. You can't "gift" shares under a wink-wink agreement that they'll sell and funnel the cash back to you just to borrow their lower bracket. A sham gift isn't a gift.

Catches & Caveats

Long-term only

The 0% rate is for long-term gains — generally investments held more than one year. Short-term gains are taxed like ordinary income, at your regular rate.

Your other income eats the bracket

Salary, interest, pension income, taxable retirement withdrawals, Roth conversions, and the like consume some or all of your 0% room first. You can't earn $300,000 from a job and then stack another $98,900 of tax-free gains on top.

Qualified dividends count too

Qualified dividends generally share these same preferential brackets, so they also consume your 0% space before your harvested gains get there.

The gain still counts as income for other purposes

Even at a 0% federal rate, the realized gain raises your AGI/MAGI. That can shrink ACA health-insurance subsidies, increase how much of your Social Security is taxable, and (years later) push up Medicare IRMAA surcharges. Harvest with your whole tax picture in view.

State taxes are separate

A 0% federal rate does not mean your state charges nothing. Some states tax capital gains as ordinary income. Check your state's rules.

The limits change yearly

The dollar thresholds are inflation-adjusted every year. Re-verify the current figures before each harvest — don't hard-code 2026 into your 2029 plan.

The honest bottom line

The 0% federal long-term capital-gains bracket is absolutely real, and for a retired couple with little other income the relevant number in 2026 isn't "$98,900 of stock" — it's roughly $131,100 of long-term profit, which (depending on basis) can mean selling several hundred thousand dollars of stock. Run your actual numbers and talk to a professional before you act.

Glossary

Long-term capital gain (LTCG)
Profit on an asset held more than one year. Eligible for the preferential 0% / 15% / 20% rates.
Short-term capital gain (STCG)
Profit on an asset held one year or less. Taxed at ordinary income rates — not eligible for the 0% bracket.
Cost basis
What you paid for an investment (plus adjustments). Your gain is sale price minus basis.
Proceeds
The total dollars you receive from a sale — not the same as your gain.
Realized vs. unrealized gain
Unrealized = on paper, you still hold it. Realized = you sold and the gain is now a taxable event.
Taxable income
Your income after deductions. The 0% capital-gains thresholds are measured against this number.
Standard deduction
A flat amount that reduces taxable income (2026: $16,100 single, $32,200 MFJ, $24,150 HoH). It applies before the 0% bracket, which is why your effective tax-free gain room is larger than the bracket alone.
AGI / MAGI
Adjusted Gross Income and Modified AGI — summary income figures used to determine eligibility for credits, ACA subsidies, Social Security taxation, and Medicare IRMAA. A harvested gain raises them even if taxed at 0%.
Qualified dividends
Dividends taxed at the same preferential rates as long-term gains — so they consume 0% bracket space too.
NIIT (Net Investment Income Tax)
An extra 3.8% tax on investment income for higher earners, layered on top of the regular capital-gains rate.
Wash-sale rule
Disallows a loss if you rebuy a substantially identical security within 30 days. Generally doesn't block gain harvesting, because there's no loss to disallow.
Carryover (gift) basis
When you gift appreciated stock, the recipient generally inherits your cost basis and holding period — the embedded gain goes with it.
Step-up in basis
By contrast, assets passed at death generally get basis reset to date-of-death value — wiping out the embedded gain. (Gifts during life do not get this.)
Holding period
How long you've owned an asset; more than a year makes a gain long-term. Gifts generally tack on the donor's holding period.
Kiddie tax
Rules that can tax a child's/young student's unearned income at the parents' rates — can apply to certain people under 18, 18, or full-time students 19–23 who don't support themselves with earned income.
Gift tax annual exclusion
The amount you can give one person per year with no gift-tax reporting (2026: $19,000 per giver; $38,000 for a couple splitting).
Lifetime exemption
The cumulative amount you can give/bequeath above annual exclusions before gift/estate tax applies (2026: $15,000,000 per person).
Form 709
The federal gift-tax return a donor files when a gift to someone exceeds the annual exclusion. Usually just tracks use of the lifetime exemption — not a tax bill.
IRMAA
Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount — Medicare surcharges triggered when income (MAGI) crosses thresholds, assessed on a ~2-year lookback.
Tax-gain harvesting
Deliberately realizing long-term gains in the 0% bracket (often to reset basis). The subject of this guide.
Tax-loss harvesting
The mirror strategy: realizing losses to offset gains/income. Subject to the wash-sale rule.
